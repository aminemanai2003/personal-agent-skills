A single composite score can make a claims-reserving benchmark easier to rank, but it can also conceal the distinctions that matter most in practice. Predictive accuracy, calibration, stability across accident years, and computational cost answer different questions. Accuracy measures how closely forecasts match observed outcomes under a chosen loss function. Calibration asks whether predicted reserve distributions or expected claims are systematically aligned with realized experience. Stability across accident years indicates sensitivity to shifts in mix, maturity, inflation, reporting patterns, and other temporal effects. Computational cost determines whether estimation, validation, scenario analysis, and production refreshes are operationally feasible. Strong performance on one dimension does not imply strength on the others.

Combining these quantities requires normalization and weighting, neither of which is neutral. A weighted composite embeds value judgments in its weights: increasing the weight on accuracy implicitly accepts more instability or expense, while emphasizing stability may reward a model that reacts slowly to genuine changes in claims dynamics. Even apparently modest choices about scaling can dominate the ranking when metrics have different ranges or tail behavior.

Consequently, two models can receive the same total score while having materially different risk profiles. One might deliver low average error but produce volatile reserve revisions across accident years; another might be well calibrated and stable but less responsive to emerging trends. Their equal totals do not make them interchangeable. The first may create earnings and capital volatility, whereas the second may allow adverse development to surface later. A computationally intensive model may also be acceptable for an annual strategic exercise but unsuitable for frequent portfolio monitoring.

Model selection should therefore begin with the intended decision, not the leaderboard. A model used for statutory reserving, capital planning, pricing feedback, or rapid operational monitoring faces different consequences of error and delay. The preferred model depends on those consequences and on the organization’s tolerance for reserve volatility. Composite scores can summarize evidence, but they should support, rather than replace, explicit examination of metric-level performance, trade-offs, and failure modes.
