A single composite score can obscure the choice a claims-reserving benchmark is meant to inform because its components measure different kinds of performance. Predictive accuracy asks how closely estimates track observed outcomes under a loss function. Calibration asks whether predicted reserve levels or distributions are systematically too high or too low. Stability across accident years tests sensitivity to shifts in development maturity, mix, inflation, and sparse historical experience. Computational cost concerns whether training, validation, retraining, and production inference are feasible within operational constraints. None of these questions is interchangeable with another, and improvement on one dimension can coincide with deterioration on another.

Combining them requires weights, scaling rules, and arbitrary transformations. Those choices are not merely technical conveniences: they encode judgments about the relative cost of bias, variance, temporal instability, and resource use. A model can therefore rank first because the scoring rule rewards the characteristics its designers happened to prioritize, not because it is uniformly preferable. The aggregation also destroys information. Two models may receive the same total score even when one is highly accurate but unstable across accident years, while the other is moderately accurate, well calibrated, and operationally cheap. Their totals match, but their implications for reserve risk, capital planning, governance, and monitoring differ materially.

Model selection should begin with the decision the reserve estimate will support. A model used for quarterly financial reporting may warrant stronger penalties for accident-year instability and systematic miscalibration, since reserve revisions can flow into earnings and capital. A model used for exploratory segmentation or rapid scenario analysis may justify accepting some volatility in exchange for speed or sharper local predictions. Composite scores can still summarize a benchmark, but they should not replace the underlying metric profile, sensitivity analysis over plausible weights, or explicit constraints on unacceptable behavior. The preferred model is conditional on purpose and on the institution’s tolerance for reserve volatility, not an intrinsic property revealed by a single number.
